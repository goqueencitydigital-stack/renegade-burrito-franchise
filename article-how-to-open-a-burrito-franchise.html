How to open a burrito franchise: a step-by-step guide

Opening a burrito restaurant is a big decision. Franchising can shorten the learning curve by giving you an established menu, systems, and support, but it is still a business you will run every day. This guide walks through the typical steps of opening a fast-casual Mexican franchise and the questions worth asking along the way.

Step 1: Take an honest look at your goals

Before you talk to any franchisor, decide what you want. Are you planning to operate the restaurant yourself, or hire a general manager? Do you want one location or several? How much of your own capital are you prepared to commit, and how much financing will you need? Your answers shape which brands, formats, and markets make sense.

Restaurant ownership rewards people who enjoy leading teams, solving problems in real time, and caring about guests. Experience in food service or multi-unit management helps, though many franchise systems train owners who come from other industries.

Step 2: Research brands and formats

Fast-casual Mexican concepts vary widely. Some focus on lunch and dinner; others, like Renegade Burrito, serve breakfast burritos all day. Some operate only traditional storefronts; others also run carry-out kitchens or concession stands at venues. Compare:

  • Menu and daypart mix
  • Restaurant formats and typical footprints
  • How long the brand has operated and how many locations it has
  • Who leads the company and what experience they bring
  • The support provided before and after opening

Step 3: Request information and have a first conversation

Most franchisors start with a short inquiry form followed by a call. Use that call to learn about the brand and to share your background. Expect to be asked about your experience, timeline, preferred market, and general financial readiness.

Step 4: Review the Franchise Disclosure Document

In the United States, the Federal Trade Commission’s Franchise Rule requires franchisors to give prospective franchisees a Franchise Disclosure Document (FDD) at least 14 calendar days before the prospect signs a binding agreement or pays any money (FTC Franchise Rule; 16 CFR Part 436). The FDD contains 23 required items, including the initial fees, estimated initial investment, ongoing fees, the franchisor’s litigation history, and audited financial statements.

Read it carefully and review it with a franchise attorney and an accountant. Some states have additional registration or disclosure requirements.

Step 5: Talk to current franchisees

The FDD lists current and former franchisees. Call them. Ask about training, support, supply chain, marketing, and what they wish they had known before opening.

Step 6: Attend a Discovery Day

Many franchisors invite qualified candidates to meet the leadership team, tour restaurants, and spend time in the kitchen. It is a chance for both sides to decide whether the fit is right.

Step 7: Secure financing and select a site

Once approved, you will work on financing and site selection. Your franchisor typically reviews and approves proposed sites against its criteria. Lease negotiation, permitting, and build-out timelines vary by market.

Step 8: Train, build, and open

Training usually combines classroom and in-restaurant work. While you train, your restaurant is built out and equipped. Opening support varies by brand — ask exactly what will be on-site with you during your first weeks. At Renegade Burrito, the FDD describes a 21-day program in the Denver metro area with 30 classroom hours and 124 on-the-job hours, and typical timing of 9 to 12 months from starting a site search to opening (Item 11).
